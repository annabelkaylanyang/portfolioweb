Overview

TL;DR: I turned my frustration with café ratings into Crema, a coffee discovery app that helps people find cafés based on their actual taste preferences.

I’m always craving a good cup of coffee with nutty, dark-chocolate notes, but trying a new café still always feels like a gamble. I’ve honestly lost count of how many highly-rated cafes that had coffee too sour or fruity for my taste. My finding was that a five-star rating might tell me how good a cafe is, but not whether I’d actually like the coffee.

My Role

What began as an individual prototype evolved into a collaborative project with two other designers. I primarily owned the community experience and led the development of Crema's visual design system, bringing the three core flows together into a cohesive high-fidelity experience.

The Problem

Finding cafés is easy. Finding coffee you'll actually like isn't.

An interview with another coffee drinker echoed my frustration. Looking at existing platforms helped us understand why.

Google Maps and Yelp make it fast and easy to find highly rated cafés, but reviews there often focus on the overall experience. Meanwhile, specialty coffee apps offer more detailed coffee information, but conversations about taste happen separately on platforms like Reddit. The information is all out there, yet coffee lovers are left piecing it together themselves.

Enthusiasts piece it together through three kinds of tools.

We explored the different parts of the coffee experience that coffee drinkers have access to now.

Opportunity

“What if café discovery was built around what you actually like, rather than what everyone else rates highly?”

Where it started

We narrowed Crema down to three core experiences.

Crema began as my solo prototype exploring taste-based café discovery. When it grew into a team project, we had five weeks to decide which parts of the concept mattered the most.

We focused on three experiences, each addressing a different need:

The early interview reinforced how much people wanted to learn from others, but I found that coffee advice in the real world remained scattered across forums and tutorials. We put features like side-by-side café comparisons and taste history on hold to focus on bringing these three experiences together.

Decision 01: Home

Our home screen was doing too much.

Our first design packed personalized recommendations, nearby cafes, friend activity, and community discussion, all into one scrolling page. We wanted to provide different avenues for people to explore coffee, but everything was competing for attention.

The Decision: Separate recommendations by intent.

Instead of mixing everything together, we divided Home into three views: For You, focused on personalized coffee recommendations, Friends, showing where people in your circle are drinking, and Local/Global, for when you’re trying to find a new local cafe or travelling and looking for spots in a new city.

This gave each recommendation a clearer purpose and let us prioritize the information relevant to each experience.

The Tradeoff

Separating the feeds reduced competition for attention, but introduced an extra step to explore Friends. During prototype walkthroughs, people tended to stay on the default tab, leaving us with an important question: would they discover the Friends experience on their own?

Separating recommendations helped people find a café. But for enthusiasts, the coffee they brew at home raises its own questions.

Decision 02: Community

Coffee questions are hard to answer without the setup.

On existing coffee forums, people often have to ask for more details before they can help: What beans are you using? What's your grinder? How are you brewing it??

I wanted Crema to make those conversations more useful from the start.

01 / Every question should already have the context it needs.

Instead of making users type out their brewing setup every time, I designed the question flow to pull saved details from their profile, such as beans, equipment, and recipe.

Users can choose which details to attach before posting, giving others the context they need while keeping control over what they share.

02 / Turn answers into something people can actually try.

Rather than relying entirely on written advice, I designed a way for answers to highlight specific brewing adjustments, such as changing grind size or extraction time.

Members can see what someone recommends changing, compare it with their current setup, and indicate whether they've tried the advice.

The Tradeoff

While structure can make advice easier to follow, it also risks making a casual conversation feel technical. To keep Community approachable, I kept free-text replies available alongside the detailed brewing suggestions.

The more Crema knows about someone's coffee preferences and brewing setup, the more relevant it can become. But when should we ask people for that information?

Decision 03: Taste Profile

We asked for preferences before showing why they mattered.

Our original onboarding started with four questions about coffee preferences before users could explore a single café. It gave Crema a starting point for personalized recommendations, but required people to invest time before experiencing the app.

The same coffee drinker from our first interview told me he wouldn't want to complete a questionnaire, even if it meant getting better recommendations.

That challenged our assumption that personalization needed to happen before discovery.

The decision: Let people explore first, personalize later.

I redesigned onboarding around a short introduction to Crema instead of a mandatory taste questionnaire. The questions moved to a dedicated Coffee Taste section in the user's profile, where they could complete them whenever they wanted. Rather than collecting everything upfront, Crema could start with nearby cafés and ask for more information when it became relevant.

The tradeoff

Removing the questionnaire makes it possible to explore immediately, but recommendations are less personalized at first. By explaining what recommendations are based on and offering optional ways to improve them, the redesign aims to make personalization feel worthwhile rather than required.

Visual Direction

Serious about coffee shouldn't mean serious-looking.

Crema started with bright blue colors and handwritten typography. It felt playful and approachable, but as the product developed, we wanted the coffee itself to take center stage. A teammate and I pushed for a more neutral direction, and I led its translation into the high-fidelity designs.

Finding the balance between clarity and personality.

01 / Playful exploration

Our original direction was expressive and energetic, but competed with the detailed coffee information we wanted to highlight.

02 / A more minimal approach

We simplified the interface and gave photography more space. But the first high-fidelity pass felt too generic, losing some of what made Crema feel distinct.

03 / Bringing personality back

We kept the clean foundation while reintroducing playful details in smaller, more intentional moments.

A visual system with a purpose.

I developed a consistent design system across the three flows, using typography to distinguish between interface content, technical coffee information, and personal expression.

Reflection

What's useful for the product isn't always what users want.

We originally included a taste questionnaire because we assumed people would want better recommendations. I didn't question it until an interviewee stopped me mid-explanation to say he wouldn't want to fill one out. It made me realize how easily we can overlook friction in parts of an experience that seem obvious to us.

Two connected experiences don't always have the same audience.

We spent a lot of time bringing café discovery and coffee discussions into one cohesive experience. Looking back, though, someone who loves exploring cafés might have no interest in brewing at home, and vice versa. If I were to continue Crema, I'd spend more time understanding these different users before deciding how central each experience should be.
